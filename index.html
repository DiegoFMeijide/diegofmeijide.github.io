NBER Working Paper No. 34851 · 2026

Does Generative AI Narrow Education-Based Productivity Gaps? Evidence from a Randomized Experiment

With Guillermo Cruces, Sebastian Galiani, Ramiro H. Gálvez, and María Lombardi

Read working paper ↗

Does generative artificial intelligence (AI) reinforce or reduce productivity differences across workers? Existing evidence largely studies AI within firms and occupations, where organizational selection compresses educational heterogeneity, leaving unclear whether AI narrows productivity gaps across individuals with different levels of education. A related question is whether the productivity gains from AI reflect productive use of the tool or mere delegation, fading once AI is unavailable, and whether the answer differs across education groups. We address both questions using a randomized online experiment outside firms, in which 1,174 adults aged 25–45 complete an incentivized, workplace-style business problem-solving task with or without a generative-AI assistant, followed by a non-AI-assisted follow-up module. AI increases performance for all participants, with substantially larger gains for lower-education individuals. In the control group without AI, higher-education participants outperform lower-education participants by 0.548 standard deviations; with AI, this gap falls to 0.139 standard deviations, closing three-quarters of the initial gap. Chat-log measures help interpret both why this gap narrows and why it does not disappear: lower-education participants successfully obtain substantial assistance from AI, while higher-education participants use the tool more effectively across several margins. The follow-up results show that these gains are not purely driven by delegation; treated participants do not perform worse than controls once AI is removed, and lower-education participants retain part of their gain, although a sizable education gap re-emerges. Consistent with this interpretation, intensive use of the assistant produces strong task performance regardless of how much time and effort participants devote to the task, but follow-up performance is substantially higher only when intensive AI use is combined with sustained effort on the task. We interpret these findings as evidence that generative AI narrows effective productivity differences in task execution while underlying human-capital differences continue to shape unassisted performance and effective use of the tool.

CAF report chapter · 2025 · pp. 77–119

EdTech and AI in Education: Transformative Potential and Challenges

With Florencia Buccari

Read chapter ↗

Translation of the original Spanish abstract.

This document examines the potential of digital technologies and artificial intelligence (AI) to improve the reach and quality of education in Latin America and the Caribbean (LAC). Although countries in the region have made progress in educational coverage, significant challenges in learning persist. In this context, EdTech interventions and the incorporation of AI offer important opportunities: in addition to helping close gaps in school performance, they can expand access for students in remote areas, facilitate the inclusion of people with disabilities, transform learning environments and design, and promote the development of digital skills. However, realizing these benefits depends on the preparedness of schools, teachers, and students in the region in terms of access to ICTs and the development of digital skills. This document shows that, despite progress in recent decades, the region continues to face major challenges in educational infrastructure and access to devices and the internet, in some cases exacerbating socioeconomic inequalities. Significant gaps in the digital skills of both students and teachers are also identified. In addition to these conditions, the document highlights the challenges associated with implementing technologies that must be considered to avoid unintended effects. In the case of EdTech interventions, it is necessary to assess their impact on interactions between students and teachers, as well as on students’ attention and physical and mental well-being. For AI applications, changes in approaches to learning and assessment must be considered, with care taken to ensure that they do not harm educational outcomes. It is also crucial to ensure the quality of content and mitigate potential biases in algorithms. Finally, the document addresses the implications for the privacy and security of the data collected by these tools.

Original title: EdTech e IA en educación: potencial transformador y desafíos

ALPHA · 2023 · 2(57), pp. 235–251

Disputes between children from different social groups: argumentative complexity, evaluative and evidential resources

With Maia Julieta Migdalek, Celia Renata Rosemberg, and Martha Shiro

Read article ↗

Argumentation is a type of discourse in which a speaker defends a claim in a discussion. The present work focuses on the argumentative complexity and the evaluative and evidential resources that characterize the disputes of children of different socioeconomic levels. We analyzed 94 disputes between 4-year-old children and other children (5 to 10 years old) in situations of spontaneous play registered in homes of middle and low socioeconomic status (SES). The analysis identified marginal differences in argumentative complexity between the groups. On the other hand, significant differences were found in evaluative expressions of emotion and in deontic and exclamatory evidential markers, more frequently used by the low SES group. The mid SES group tends to use a higher number of expressions of causality and connectors. The results are discussed with reference to the findings of previous research.

Original title: Disputas entre niños de distintos grupos sociales: complejidad argumentativa, evaluación y evidencialidad

Master's thesis · 2026

The Art of (Automatic) Grading: Using Language Models to Evaluate Texts

Master in Public Policy · Universidad Torcuato Di Tella

Advisor: María Lombardi

Read thesis ↗

Translation of the original Spanish abstract.

This thesis evaluates the performance of Large Language Models (LLMs) as automated graders of written responses in an online experiment conducted in Argentina in 2025 (Cruces et al., 2026). The dataset includes 1174 participants who responded to a fictitious workplace email from a supervisor. Their responses were scored on two dimensions, content and writing, on a scale from 0 to 10. This study measures within-model consistency through ten independent grading runs per response and the calculation of intraclass correlations. Second, it compares the ordering produced by different evaluation sources, including GPT, Claude, Gemini, and variants using alternative rubrics and ranking schemes. Finally, LLM performance is validated against a human benchmark in a subsample of 117 responses graded by two evaluators. The results show high stability and strong agreement across models and methods, and with human evaluators, suggesting that, with appropriate calibration and supervision, LLMs can support large-scale text evaluation processes in public policy contexts.
